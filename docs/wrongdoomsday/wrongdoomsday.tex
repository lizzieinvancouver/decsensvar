\begin{enumerate}
\item Introduction
\begin{enumerate}
\item Climate change is shifting biological systems globally with potential impacts on ecosystem stability and the global climate system itself \citep{richardson2023earth}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Shifted phenology---the timing of recurring life history events---has been the clearest `fingerprint' of anthropogenic climate change across both natural and managed systems
\item ... with major impacts.... 
\item Spring frosts are increasing, with major impacts on forests \citep{gu2008} and major crops (e.g., winegrapes)
\item Additionally, there are increasing reports of `abnormal seasonal activities' (\url{https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-16585-2})
\item Uneven shifts across some trophic levels has driven concern of declining pollination and other ecosystem services with continued warming
\item Most concerns of trophic mismatch premise that different trophic levels use different cues to time life history events and thus may become desynchronized with continued warming, but synchrony within a species or population is also often critical
\end{enumerate}
\item Here we show how warming may be desynchronizing spring plant phenology (flower and leaf-burst, `budburst') without any shift or difference in the cues. 
\begin{enumerate}
\item Using the simplest model---a thermal sum accumulation model, which is underlying model for many phenological events across plants and insects---we show how the pattern of temperatures predicts increasing variance with continued warming
\item Our main finding is that the linear increase of average temperatures, which is definitional to climatological `spring' months makes plants budburst at the same time, with variation between individuals negligible.  But as temperatures warm, budburst happens in the more stationary temperature regimes (where average temperatures do not increase) and small variations have a larger effect, resulting in the desynchronization of blooms or a scattered spring.
\end{enumerate}
\item by shifting phenological triggers (thresholds?) earlier into more stationary seasonal climate periods 
\end{enumerate}
\item Explaining spring versus winter ... 
\begin{enumerate}
\item This theory suggests the synchrony between buds depends critically on whether temperatures accumulate predominately in relatively flat or stationary weather periods or during periods of day over day warming periods. 
\item Such static or stationary weather regimes generally define the traditional winter  (January - March, and summer months: June-August) while warming defines the spring (March - May), though shorter periods of either regime type can happen generally in any month
\end{enumerate}

\section{Outline: 26 August 2025 -- tipping point} % How climate change breaks a fundamental model of plant biology to degrade plant forecasting

\begin{enumerate}
\item Introduction
\begin{enumerate}
\item Climate change is shifting biological systems globally with potential impacts on ecosystem stability and the global climate system itself \citep{richardson2023earth}
\begin{enumerate}
\item The most observed and well documented biological impact is shifts in phenology----the timing of recurring life history events \citep{ipcc2022,Menzel1999,Root:2003kl}
\item Shifted phenology has been the clearest `fingerprint' of anthropogenic climate change, it may also be the one to first show evidence that systems are no longer responding to climate change as they did in the past \citep{piao2019plant,vitasse2022great}
\item Such a phase change or tipping point \citep{Dakos2013} have been seen in other human-altered systems (but understanding why they happen and predicting them has been difficult) \citep{Chavez2003}
\item Understanding and predicting this has big consequences for global carbon models (will plants sequester more carbon?), economic models of crops (peach frost etc.) and more... \citep{chamberlain2021climate,zohner2020late,lamichhane2021rising}
\end{enumerate}
\item Research is divided on whether phenological responses are showing hints of new responses \citep{fu2015declining,gusewell2017changes} or not \citep{wolkovich2021simple}, which may because we lack an underlying model
\begin{enumerate}
\item Declining sensitivities, or not
\item Increasing variance, or not
\item Related to this, are trees growing more with longer seasons, or not (or maybe we just focus on phenology) \citep{dow2022warm,keenan2014net}
\item These debates stem from lacking an underlying model that we all agree on (and we don't have great data)
\end{enumerate}
\item Here we show how shifted autocorrelation in climate (or do we want to call it `biological climate' or something to represent that it's the GDD metric? Though I am not a fan of new names/terms) due to anthropogenic climate change predicts a breakdown of one of the most fundamental models of plant biology and show how it may lead to a decline in forecasting accuracy .... 
\item We show results with lilacs
\item And with other data
\item Our results suggest climate change has fundamentally disrupted the central limit theory for one of the most important biological models with consequences for higher variance and lower predictability from crops to forests and likely extends to upper trophic levels as well. 
\end{enumerate}
